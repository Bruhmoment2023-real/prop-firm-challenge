## 2. What each one already does (from the full abstracts; claims about the body are unverified)
- **Villahermosa.** Driftless pass probability L/(T+L) by optional stopping, independent of volatility, shown to be a ceiling. Six years of one-minute index-futures data: 44.4% bare geometry, 40.4% with daily bracket, 34.7% with trailing floor. Life-cycle expected value: +0.052 L at the fee paid, -0.018 L at list price. Seller break-even pass rate 20% (fee paid) or 40% (list); mechanical participant 34.7%. States the economics assume no directional edge and are a lower bound for the buyer. Nothing in the abstract about inferring skill from a pass.
- **Tomas Fernandez.** Closed-form pass probability as a chain of first-passage problems with six contract parameters, error under 1% against simulation; 13 products, 62% spread in success probability at identical advertised target and buffer. Derives a minimum productivity and a "break-even conversion rate"; says published pass rates lie below it and the margin comes from "the selection the evaluation performs". This is the closest to a selection argument: it speaks of selection and implied buyer drift, but the abstract gives no likelihood ratio or posterior that a passer is skilled.
- **Lim.** Two-step contract as a contingent claim, Monte Carlo first passage: zero-edge pass 0.50 frictionless vs 0.30 with the contract's time limit; value ridge near 2% daily volatility; model-implied average value USD 221 of a USD 500 challenge; 7.5% of simulated buyers reach funded status; proposes disclosure. Nothing about skill inference.
- **Hall (full text searched).** Table 7 gives P(pass | skill q = mu/sigma) at several cadences and sizing rules; zero skill with optimised sizing passes 0.434 versus 0.101 at fixed sizing, and a real edge q = 0.05 at fixed sizing raises it to 0.394, so "the pass rate confounds skill with sizing at roughly equal weight". Hall states the evaluation "is a filter" and asks "how strong a filter, and against what", but a keyword search of the 40 pages finds no likelihood ratio, no posterior probability for base rates, and no optimal-test benchmark. (Searches for "likelihood", "posterior", "Bayes", "base rate", "Neyman": the only "likelihood" hits are about the likelihood of receiving a payoff in a shrouded-attributes argument.)

## 3. Does "how much does a pass reveal about skill" still stand?
- **Partly narrowed, not removed.** The forward direction P(pass | skill) now exists in print (Hall Table 7; Lim and Villahermosa for the zero-skill ceiling). What none of the four abstracts or the Hall text shows is the inverse: P(skill | pass), i.e. a likelihood ratio of a pass, posterior probabilities under stated base rates of skilled traders (the base rates themselves come from the Barber et al. and Chague et al. day-trading literature), and the comparison with the best possible test using the same number of trades.
- **What is verified:** Hall's text has the forward gradient and the sizing confound; the keyword search found none of the inverse items in it.
- **What is unverified:** whether Tomas Fernandez's body text (its "selection" argument and "break-even conversion rate") contains a Bayesian or likelihood-ratio statement, and whether Villahermosa's or Lim's do. Their abstracts suggest not, but the bodies were not read. Sean must open all three before claiming the gap; SSRN downloads by hand are the way.
- **Safe framing for planning (a position, not prose):** the claim should be "a pass-versus-skill inference with base rates and an optimal-test benchmark", not "first to show a pass is weak evidence of skill"; the latter is already stated by Hall in the abstract.
- **Replication value is higher than before:** four independent zero-edge numbers (0.30, 0.347, 0.40, 0.36-0.47) now need reconciling on one rule set, which supports contribution 2 in `01-prior-work.md`.
